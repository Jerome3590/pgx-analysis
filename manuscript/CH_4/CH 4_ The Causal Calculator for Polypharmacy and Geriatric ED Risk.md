**Chapter 4: Polypharmacy, Drug Interactions, and Causal Rules**  
**4.1 Introduction**The crisis of polypharmacy and adverse drug events in older adult populations presents a significant and growing challenge to healthcare systems 1\. As the number of prescribed medications increases with age, so does the risk of complex, unforeseen compounding effects that can lead to severe clinical deterioration. In recent years, advanced machine learning (ML) frameworks have offered enhanced predictive capabilities due to their robust handling of categorical variables, improved management of multiple interactive variables, and non-linear relationships 2\. However, merely predicting risk is insufficient for clinical intervention; providers need transparent, causal explanations for *why* a risk exists. Therefore, this chapter addresses the following core research question: **"How can Formal Feature Attribution (FFA) be applied as a 'causal calculator' to identify synergistic and antagonistic polypharmacy drug interactions that precipitate non-opioid, high-cost geriatric ED visits?"** 1\.  
**4.2 Cohort Definition & The Causal Calculator Constraint**To isolate the effects of polypharmacy, we defined a specific Polypharmacy/Non-Opioid ED Cohort, targeting high-cost/geriatric condition ED visits 3, 4\. The analysis focuses on older adult age bands, specifically stratifying patients into 65–74, 75–84, and 85–114 years 3, 4\. The cohort was constructed utilizing a 5:1 ratio of control to target cases 5, with rigorous temporal validation ensuring robust generalization: models were trained on data from 2016–2018 and evaluated on a 2019 holdout set, excluding 2020 to prevent confounding from COVID-19 healthcare disruptions 6\.  
A unique and critical constraint of this modeling phase is the strict restriction of the feature space. Unlike broader clinical models, all medical diagnosis and procedure codes were stripped from the data 3\. The feature space was restricted purely to drug names, drug counts, and Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) counts 3\. By forcing the algorithm to learn exclusively from medication exposure, the model functions strictly as a "drug interaction causal calculator," ensuring that the resulting explanations are driven by pharmacology rather than underlying disease states 3\.  
**4.3 Building and Evaluating the Risk Models**Because patients with very few medical events (low density) have fundamentally different clinical profiles than highly active patients (extreme density), utilizing a single full-cohort model typically pulls the decision boundary toward the dominant group, underperforming on the tails 7\. To address this, we developed **per-bin models** stratified by event density (low, medium, high, extreme) 7, 8\.  
We utilized a Monte Carlo cross-validation approach to screen feature importance 9\. For final model training, four candidate algorithms were evaluated per cohort/age-band bin: XGBoost, XGBoost in Random Forest (RF) mode, CatBoost, and a probability-average Ensemble 10\. CatBoost and XGBoost were selected due to their advanced handling of categorical variables and validated superior performance in complex healthcare datasets 11, 12\. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using Bayesian optimization via the Optuna framework, which searches for parameters that provide the best cross-validated predictive performance 13\. Models were rigorously calibrated using Platt scaling 8 and evaluated based primarily on their Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC) mean—ensuring safety for imbalanced classes—with Recall mean acting as a secondary tiebreaker 10, 14\.  
**4.4 Multi-Feature Combinatorial Analysis (FFA Pipeline)**To translate the predictive models into interpretable clinical insights, we executed a Formal Feature Attribution (FFA) Analysis (Step 8 of the pipeline) 15, 16\. While SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were first calculated globally and locally to identify the most important baseline features and directional impacts 16, 17, SHAP alone does not adequately capture the multi-drug causal rules.  
The FFA pipeline utilizes the best-performing XGBoost variant (xgb or xgb\_rf) to generate symbolic rule explanations 15, 16\. Relying on SHAP-based filtering to prioritize the most robust rules, the FFA causal pipeline tests combinations of features—specifically pairs and triplets of drugs—without restricting the search space to an arbitrary top-K limit 15\. This methodology systematically calculates final causal importance scores and extracts precise Boolean logic rules to explain risk 15, 18, outputting the findings into structural datasets (causal\_importance.parquet and interaction\_analysis.parquet) 15, 16\.  
**4.5 Synergy vs. Antagonism in Drug Interactions**The clinical insights generated by the FFA rules provide a nuanced map of polypharmacy risk. By analyzing the generated causal rules, the model successfully identifies two primary system archetypes affecting the geriatric population:

* **Positive Synergy:** Specific drug-drug interactions that compound to causally *increase* the risk of a high-cost ED visit beyond the sum of their individual risks 3, 18\.  
* **Negative Antagonism:** Drug combinations that interact to neutralize risk or provide a protective clinical effect 3, 18\.

To make these findings accessible, the FFA Boolean logic rules are integrated with FP-Growth process mining to visually map drug co-occurrence networks, highlighting the most frequent and dangerous drug combinations triggering geriatric ED admissions 18, 19\.
